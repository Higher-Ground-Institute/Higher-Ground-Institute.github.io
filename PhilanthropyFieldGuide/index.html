Who funds AI’s future, and what they believe

A field guide to the foundations and new AI wealth shaping AI policy. It’s a companion to HGI’s AI political field guide, built to help navigate funders’ approaches and beliefs the way that political guide does with the political factions.

Kate Gage, Higher Ground Institute

Why a second guide

The political guide sorts people by coalition. Funders sort by what they think AI will do to institutions and where they think a dollar changes the outcome. This guide reads grants and open calls as evidence of those beliefs.

What they believe

Is AI a risk to contain, a tool to deploy, or a shift in power?

Who they fund

Researchers, civil society, government, or builders.

Which way they push

Slow it down, steer it, or speed it up.

How to spot them

Vocabulary gives them away. The decoder below sorts it by camp.

What the grants show

Four findings that don’t fit the two-sides story about AI money.

  • The same foundations fund both sides

    Packard and Hewlett fund the accountability groups; Knight funds information and civic-capacity research; all three fund the center-right Foundation for American Innovation, which in turn helps steer AI safety grants.

  • Most new funds trace back to the same few foundations

    Four of the five newer international funds traced back to the same funders: Omidyar, Ford, MacArthur, Hewlett and Open Society. Omidyar alone sits behind all four.

  • AI profits now pay for AI safety

    The Navigation Fund is funded by the AI computing company it started, now merged with Lightning AI in a $2.5B deal.

  • Populist organizing is supported by safety-aligned groups

    AI Populist money mostly moves through political committees such as Guardrails and Tomorrow Together, and MAGA Populists have no philanthropic base of their own. Much of the organizing aimed at populist audiences is supported by safety-aligned groups instead. The Pro-Human AI Coalition was co-founded by the Future of Life Institute, Frank McCourt’s Project Liberty and Ergo Impact.

Migration pathways

Grants from the past two years show three shifts. All three end up at the same question: who holds power as AI spreads.

EthicsSecurity

Legacy foundations are moving toward national security

Hewlett co-founded the 2017 Ethics and Governance of AI Fund. In 2026 it launched a $100M security initiative.

CatastrophePower

Safety funders now ask who controls AI

Longview ran a 2026 call on extreme power concentration. SFF added new Freedom and Fairness tracks. An addition, not a swap: the catastrophic-risk concern hasn’t softened.

WatchdogDelivery

Democracy funders are betting on government that works

Packard funds government capacity alongside accountability. Rockefeller now co-funds with the OpenAI Foundation.

Where the three shifts meet

Packard’s field report points to “gradual disempowerment” (see the decoder) as common ground between safety funders and accountability funders. Both groups now pay for independent testing and corporate accountability.

  • Responsible Technology Youth Power Fund: Omidyar plus ten others, including McGovern and Pivotal; Encode’s funder

  • Recoding America Fund: a fund that is itself a funder, including of FAI
  • Longview and SFF: move new-wealth safety money, Tallinn’s included, into grantees like Horizon and Encode

  • Future of Life Institute: realized roughly $500M from Vitalik Buterin’s 2021 crypto gift, by his account; now funds Encode, Common Sense Media and the Institute for Family Studies
  • Omidyar’s AI Collaborative: grants still being traced; Omidyar took its grantee list off its site in 2025

Where grants become politics

Five of the grantees above have crossed from research into campaigns. FLI ran an $8M state campaign in 2026 and co-founded the pro-human coalition. ARI helped build the coalition that beat federal preemption of state AI laws, 99 to 1 in the Senate. Encode worked state bills and got subpoenaed by OpenAI for it; Omidyar, named as its funder, stopped signing public letters and pulled its grantee list. The Center for AI Safety’s c4, the CAIS Action Fund, co-sponsored California’s SB 1047 and lobbied for the US AI Safety Institute. Coefficient has started a program to train AI safety political operatives. By Politico’s tally, safety-affiliated groups outspent the industry side $44.4M to $27.9M in the 2026 midterms. That is the seam between this guide and the political one: the same foundations that fund the research now sit one step behind the PACs.

The crossover has its own critics inside the money. Vitalik Buterin said in March 2026 that FLI made “cultural and political action” its primary method after his gift, and warned that big-money political campaigns invite backlash.

“Safety”

  • Safety science: preventing catastrophe
  • Counterweights: harm to people now
  • Security: national security
  • Lab foundations: responsible rollout

“Capacity”

  • State capacity: government that delivers
  • Safety science: a pipeline of researchers
  • Counterweights: stronger civil society groups
  • Lab foundations: cities delivering services with AI (Cities Deliver)

“Public interest”

  • Counterweights: accountable to the public
  • Public builders: owned and run outside the companies
  • Lab foundations: the company’s own giving
  • State capacity: public services that work

The pro-human coalition is the first vehicle built across camps

FLI launched the Pro-Human AI Declaration in March 2026 with signatures from Yoshua Bengio, Susan Rice, Steve Bannon, Mustafa Suleyman, Ralph Nader and Daron Acemoglu. By September it had become a nonprofit coalition of 50+ organizations, co-founded by FLI, Project Liberty and Ergo Impact, with Omidyar and Mila on the steering committee and the Center for AI Safety, the Center for Humane Technology, Encode, Minderoo and Public AI as members. Its workstreams run from a “pro-human AI stack” to policy deliverables for the June 2027 Geneva AI Summit, and its members claim bipartisan AI laws in Utah, California, Ohio, Connecticut and South Dakota. That is Safety science money, Counterweights language and populist tactics in one organization. It also complicates the finding above that populists have no philanthropic base: through FLI and McCourt, they now have one.

Critics read the brand differently. Timnit Gebru’s response to the declaration: “Who is in this alliance you say? Well none other than Steve Bannon.” Reporting on the September assembly noted FLI’s own funding roots (Elon Musk’s founding $10M, Jaan Tallinn on the board, about $1M from Coefficient Giving) and that the only concrete harms discussed on stage were chatbot harms to children.

Deep dive: AI and democracy

This is where Counterweights and State capacity overlap most. The central document is Packard’s March 2026 report, AI and Democracy: Perspectives from an Emerging Field, co-authored by Kelly Born, who leads Packard’s Democracy, Rights, and Governance work.

AI is an accelerant, amplifying both strengths and weaknesses across existing systems.

Shevin, Born and Kirby, “AI and Democracy: Perspectives from an Emerging Field,” Packard, March 2026

What the report asks funders to do

  1. Build up the institutions that will absorb AI: agencies, regulators, unions, newsrooms.
  2. Pay for independent testing and measurement, instead of trusting industry’s numbers.
  3. Take on who owns AI through competition, labor and corporate governance work.

Where it sits

The report focuses on institutions rather than on the technology itself. The authors note their interviews lean toward near-term-risk funders, so this is the Counterweights and State capacity view rather than the whole field.

What the report leaves out

The report maps 130+ organizations, including safety groups like METR and Epoch. It lists no campaign or electoral infrastructure groups. Its elections category covers administration and integrity only.
