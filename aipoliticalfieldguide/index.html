Who was spending

The tracker sorts PACs as "pro-innovation" or "pro-safety." Those labels hide the factions. Here is who is behind each side and what they believe.

Tech Right · "pro-innovation"

Leading the Future network

Who: venture capital and OpenAI money. Marc Andreessen and Ben Horowitz of a16z ($25M each) and OpenAI president Greg Brockman and Anna Brockman ($25M). FEC via Transformer

Believes: AI means abundance; regulation entrenches incumbents and helps China; one federal law should preempt the states.

Vehicles: Think Big (Democrats), American Mission (Republicans), Building the Future. Allied conservative group: Alliance for a Better Future.

Record: lost every competitive general-election race it entered.

AI Safety · "pro-safety"

Public First network

Who: frontier-lab money from the safety side. Anthropic gave $40M to the Public First Action nonprofit; CEO Dario Amodei and Anthropic researchers gave to its PACs. Politico, FEC via Transformer

Believes: advanced AI could be catastrophic, so frontier labs need binding rules now, and both parties have to own the issue.

Vehicles: Jobs and Democracy (Democrats), Defending Our Values (Republicans). Allies: You Can Push Back (Ripple co-founder Chris Larsen) and Dream NYC (Anthropic, OpenAI and Jane Street staff).

Record: its Democrats won; its Republicans mostly lost.

AI Populists · "pro-safety"

The punish lane

Who: tech workers, teachers' unions and Democratic party donors. Guardrails Alliance draws on AI-industry employees, AFT and AAUP; Tomorrow Together is led by Harris 2024 alum Rob Flaherty and Karthik Ganapathy. FEC via Transformer

Believes: the problem is who controls AI, not the technology. Make AI-industry money toxic and make Democrats the party that stands up to it.

Vehicles: Guardrails Alliance, Tomorrow Together. Who Decides organizes but doesn't spend on candidates.

Record: 11 for 11 against the candidates it targeted.

The other three factions had no PAC money in play. AI Ethics has influence but no political spending. MAGA Populists showed up as the voters who turned on data centers, and as Republican candidates both safety and industry PACs courted. Pragmatists shape bill text, not campaigns.

What happened

  • The Tech Right's money lost its competitive bets

    The a16z- and Brockman-funded Leading the Future network picked three competitive general-election races and lost all three: Darline Graham in the South Carolina Senate race, Aaron Flint in MT-1 and Laurie Buckhout in NC-1 (TechCrunch). Its wins came in safe seats, plus Jared Moskowitz in FL-25. It ends the cycle with more than $20M unspent and says it is playing for 2028 (The Next Web).

  • AI Safety money split by party

    The Anthropic-funded Public First network ran a transpartisan play: Democrats through Jobs and Democracy, Republicans through Defending Our Values. Its Democrats won: Manny Rutinel (CO-8), Brian Poindexter (OH-7), Bob Brooks (PA-7) and Scott Wiener (CA-11). Its Republicans mostly lost: Carlos De La Cruz (TX-35), Rhett Marques (AL-2), Scott Singer (FL-25) and Alex Mealer (TX-9) (Fortune, Semafor). Pete Ricketts in Nebraska was the exception. The safety faction's bet on Republicans who share MAGA Populist worries about Big Tech did not pay off this year.

  • The AI Populists ran the table

    All six of Tomorrow Together's targets lost: Husted, Marshall, Ciscomani, Barrett, Van Orden and Bresnahan (Politico, CBS). All five of Guardrails Alliance's general-election targets lost: Husted, Flint, Jay Feely (AZ-1), Buckhout and Jimmy Gomez, a Democrat, in CA-34 (Transformer, Semafor).

    Read this with care. Most targets were Republicans in a wave year, with Democrats flipping more than 40 House seats (CBS), so the money and the wave are hard to separate.

  • Data centers: the populist horseshoe won at the ballot box

    Voters went against data centers in 40 of 41 local measures across 10 states, with almost 20 in Ohio. Harwood, North Dakota was the one industry win (Route Fifty). This is the fight where AI Populists and MAGA Populists line up against the Tech Right. In South Carolina, Annie Andrews beat the candidate she called "Data Center Darline" (Semafor), after the Tech Right's network put $3.2M behind Graham on FEC record (Transformer) and $4.8M by press reports (TechCrunch).

  • The Tech Right's Senate allies lost too

    Alliance for a Better Future, a pro-AI group backing Republicans, spent for Susan Collins in Maine ($810K), Mike Rogers in Michigan (about $1M) and Ashley Hinson in Iowa ($171K). All three lost (Citation Needed, Gizmodo).

  • Every faction says it's staying

    Tech Right, via LTF: "We are committed to a multi-cycle effort and 2028 will show who actually cares." AI Safety, via Public First's Brad Carson: "We showed our might." (Politico) AI Populists, via Guardrails: "Our goal is to make AI money politically toxic." (TechCrunch) The fight now moves to 2028 positioning, where Who Decides and Tomorrow Together were already aimed.

Faction profile

The sixth faction: AI Populists

Every other faction is defined by its answer to "what is AI?" This one is defined by "who has power?"

Its analysis: most people are open to AI as a technology, but not to the tech oligarchy, corruption, autocracy and climate cost that come with its current trajectory. That makes it a political-economy faction, not an AI-prediction faction.

Expect many more groups to land here. This is the accountability wing of the progressive movement arriving in AI politics, and after November it has a winning record to point to.

How it works

  • Theory of change. Near-term harms (surveillance, deepfakes, job loss) are legitimate concerns and organizing gateways to structural demands about power and wealth concentration.
  • People-power lever. The data center moratorium movement. Slowing the buildout opens the Overton window for a real policy framework. Not universally embraced: some in the camp think it is the wrong fight to attach to AI regulation.
  • Insider lever. A token tax, the emerging insider version of the same strategy: policy pressure to bring labs to the negotiating table.

Red lines: the populist lane's first policy floor

On Oct 7, 2026, nearly 40 labor, progressive, faith and AI safety groups led by Guardrails Action set the conditions any federal AI bill must meet. Signers include the American Federation of Teachers and Who Decides. Bills that cross the lines face "active, mobilized opposition."

Real oversight

Independent government oversight with enforcement power.

A broad mandate

Covers human impacts from safety to economic disruption.

Humans make the call

No final AI decisions on denying health care or benefits, firing or disciplining workers, or deploying weapons.

A universal benefit

AI's gains shared, not captured.

Same words, different sides

"Slow down" now means pace (the labs), pause (PauseAI), ban (Sanders-Casar), "nobody is stopping you" (Sacks), or self-police (Trump). Which one a person reaches for tells you whether they want the law involved.

"Superintelligence" has meant three things in twelve years. To Nick Bostrom, who put the word into circulation with his 2014 book, it is a hypothetical future system that "greatly exceeds the cognitive performance of humans in virtually all domains of interest": the thing the safety camp fears. To the Sanders-Casar bill, it is a legal threshold: AI that matches or exceeds human cognitive performance, or that can resist shutdown, and it is banned. To the Trump administration, since a Sept 29 executive order, it is the new federal name for all AI, current products included. Same word: a danger, a crime and a brand.

The political overlay: money, coalitions and flashpoints

The money (2026 cycle)

AI-focused super PACs spent $72.3M on the 2026 elections by the Transformer tracker's FEC count. The Tech Right's PACs spent $27.9M; AI Safety and AI Populist PACs spent $44.4M. Most of it went into primaries. Tech Influence Watch puts the total closer to $79M across 68 House and Senate seats.

The Tech Right's money is Leading the Future's network and Meta's PACs, with four people and one company behind almost all of LTF's money. Meta's spending is mostly state-level and is not in the federal count. AI Safety's money is Public First Action, funded by Anthropic and spending on both parties. The AI Populists' money is a cluster of smaller Democratic-aligned vehicles. Every figure, by vehicle, is in the ledger below.

First stress test, NY-12 Democratic primary. $29.5M flowed around RAISE Act author Alex Bores in an eight-candidate field. AI Safety PACs spent $21.3M for him; the Tech Right's Think Big spent $8.16M against; Bloomberg separately backed Micah Lasher, his former aide. Lasher won 39 to 35. The safety side outspent the industry side 2.6 to 1 and lost, which makes NY-12 a story about the limits of AI money generally. Bores then launched Who Decides on Sept 15 to put Democrats on a common AI agenda by 2028: "We don't let five people write the laws for hundreds of millions of Americans. There is no reason to let them write the rules for AI."

The general election. The Tech Right's network sat on its cash and lost the competitive races it entered. The late money came from AI Populist vehicles, and every candidate they targeted lost. Details are in 2026 results.

Flashpoint 1: federal preemption of state AI laws

The Tech Right wants one national framework and tried a state-law moratorium and NDAA preemption. A horseshoe coalition beat it: AI Populist Democrats Warren and Sanders alongside MAGA Populists Bannon, Greene and Hawley, and Rand Paul. The July 2025 amendment stripping the moratorium from the reconciliation bill passed 99-1, sponsored by Sens. Cantwell, Blackburn, Markey and Collins; 260+ state lawmakers from all 50 states had already opposed the moratorium, and 280 opposed the later NDAA attempt. A separate NDAA preemption attempt followed; these are distinct fights. A December 2025 executive order then created an AI Litigation Task Force to sue states anyway.

Flashpoint 2: data centers and electricity prices

Bipartisan backlash has made data centers a kitchen-table issue and utility bills a core affordability concern. 61% of voters oppose a new data center near them. In November voters went against data centers in 40 of 41 local ballot measures.

Bottom line: the AI Safety, AI Ethics and AI Populist camps hold no power in this White House. The Tech Right has money and access, but it keeps colliding with a horseshoe of AI Populists and MAGA Populists over state preemption, data centers and surveillance, and in November that horseshoe won at the ballot box.

Internal fights worth knowing

  • Within AI Safety

    Yudkowsky and MIRI purists ("shut it all down") versus Coefficient Giving and Anthropic. Anthropic is the flashpoint: hypocrite to some, best hope to others.

  • Within the right

    Tech Right versus the MAGA base is open war over preemption and now surveillance. The Tech Right's own voters are turning against data centers and rate hikes.

  • Within the left

    The ethics and safety camps compete for regulatory oxygen, and the TESCREAL fight left open wounds. The AI Populists' data center moratorium strategy is not universally embraced even inside the camp.

  • Within labor

    Building trades want data center construction jobs and resist moratorium framing. AFT and AAUP are funding the Guardrails Alliance. SAG-AFTRA and WGA are fighting a separate consent-and-compensation war over training data. The AFL-CIO has not unified these blocs into a single AI position.

  • Within the child safety coalition

    Agreement on the need for accountability; disagreement on remedies. Fairplay, Encode and Design It For Us want duty-of-care and age-assurance mandates. EFF, CDT and Fight for the Future call that an ID checkpoint on the internet and oppose KOSA and STOP CSAM outright. CDT co-signed an anti-age-verification letter with Americans for Prosperity.

Scrambler 1

Data centers

Data centers are where the factions break formation. The fight is no longer two-sided: alongside the camps that want to stop the buildout and the camps that want to accelerate it, a third group is writing the rules for how it happens. Who pays, what gets taxed, what gets disclosed, what gets enforced.

Stop

Moratorium coalition

Ethics and environmental left: 500+ groups from 47 states demand a congressional moratorium.

"Wherever these hyperscale data centers are built, electricity rates skyrocket for families and small businesses, water stability is threatened, and communities are turned upside down."Emily Wurth, Food and Water Watch

AI Populists: the moratorium is strategy, not just defense. Slowing the buildout is the one people-power lever that opens space for a real regulatory framework.

"Data centers increase residential energy bills by upwards of 250% and many communities are left in the dark on these projects from the start."Meghan Pazik, Public Citizen

MAGA populists, blasting their own administration:

"How dare the peasants complain about data centers stealing their water and driving the cost of electricity!!"Marjorie Taylor Greene, mocking reported FBI monitoring of data center protesters

Sanders and Ocasio-Cortez introduced the AI Data Center Moratorium Act; roughly a dozen states have introduced moratorium bills; New York's executive order is the first statewide pause; Maine's LD 307 passed both chambers and was vetoed; Monterey Park, CA became the first US city to permanently ban data centers by ballot measure. AI Now's North Star Data Center Policy Toolkit is written for this posture, with fallback protections where a full stop is not feasible.

Build

Tech Right, White House and the labs

The AI Action Plan's core pillar is "Build American AI Infrastructure": faster permitting, looser environmental review.

"Technology is going to happen. Trying to stop it is like ordering the tides to stop."David Sacks, former White House AI czar

Frontier labs, including the "safety" one: OpenAI wants 250 GW of capacity by 2033, "a gigawatt of new AI infrastructure every week." Anthropic committed $50B to US data centers, asking "do we have what it takes to build AI in America?" (though it pledged to shoulder ratepayer price hikes itself).

The White House Ratepayer Protection Pledge (March 2026) is the industry's answer to the affordability backlash, but it is voluntary and unenforceable, with no interconnection, financing or supply chain mechanism behind it.

There is no organized constituency for building. Polling runs roughly 30 for and 70 against in both parties, which is what happens when one side stops making a case (Jasmine Sun).

How

The rule-writers

The largest and least organized group: not pro-build, not pro-moratorium, looking for a framework. Most of the 2026 policy activity lives here, with 300+ data center bills filed across 30 states in the first six weeks of the year.

  • Who pays. Strict cost causation, special rate classes for large loads (18+ states), take-or-pay commitments and decommissioning bonds.
  • Tax. Virginia enacted a first-of-its-kind $0.011/kWh data center electricity consumption tax. Others are pausing incentives or conditioning abatements on grid connection and clean power. Federal insider variant: the token tax.
  • Environment and enforcement. Emissions get decided in rate cases, air permit hearings and enforcement: prohibit new on-site gas, require clean firm power, mandate load flexibility, register large loads with NERC. Climate Power scores the eight hyperscalers on behind-the-meter gas and local grid carbon.
  • Process and consent. The backlash is driven less by water and noise than by process failure and weak benefits: NDAs, fast tracks and being blindsided (Jasmine Sun). So: ban NDAs binding public officials (Microsoft has already conceded this), require pre-permit disclosure, and set standard terms of entry. New York's draft floats $1M in community benefits per megawatt.

The grid-leverage case: treating the buildout as yes or no is a strategic error, and there is a narrow, closing window to convert data center demand into transmission, ratepayer relief and domestic supply chains (Jane Flegal, Searchlight).

The governance case: the moratorium push is a strategic dead end with real equity costs, and the answer is democratic oversight of how AI develops (Holly Buck, Jacobin).

The live argument is stop versus how

The moratorium camp reads HOW as pre-negotiated surrender; the governance camp reads STOP as conceding the terms. Both are inside the same coalition. Schisms to watch: PauseAI protests the buildout while safety-branded Anthropic spends $50B on it. The White House fast-tracks permits while its base fights projects district by district.

Scoreboard: 61% oppose a data center near where they live, with 14% in support (Annenberg, June to July 2026); 75 projects worth roughly $130B blocked or delayed in Q1 2026 alone, matching all of 2025; active opposition groups more than doubled from 396 to 833 across 49 states; 69 local moratoria enacted by May 2026; New York, Texas and Seattle have all paused new projects. On Nov 3, voters went against data centers in 40 of 41 local ballot measures, including a ban on "high impact data centers" in Emporia, Kansas with just over 60%.

The build side is no longer only defending BUILD. Build American AI, the Tech Right's dark-money arm, ran ad campaigns in Kansas, Ohio and Wisconsin, and its PAC, Building the Future, pitched itself on protecting ratepayers and water. Tech Right money has entered the HOW column, and its one candidate lost to an opponent who ran against "Data Center Darline."

Scrambler 2

Open source AI

"Open weights" is not open source: you can download the model but not the training data or code. Critics call the gap "open washing." Each faction means something different by "open."

Tech Right

Open as weapon

a16z leads with "Asserting American Leadership in Open Source AI"; it is in Trump's AI Action Plan. The pitch: open models let little tech compete and beat China. The tell: "open source" here mostly means deregulation.

AI Safety

Open as threat

You cannot recall an open model, and guardrails can be fine-tuned away. Geoffrey Hinton compared open-sourcing big models to "being able to buy nuclear weapons at Radio Shack." This drove the SB 1047 fight (Newsom veto, Sept 2024).

Public interest left

Open as commons

Mozilla is spending from $1.4B in reserves on an open source "rebel alliance" against OpenAI and Anthropic ("owners, not renters"), with the Public AI movement pushing publicly funded models and compute. But AI Now warns "open" models still run on Big Tech's compute.

Geopolitics

The China effect

DeepSeek (Jan 2025) made open models a geopolitical weapon. Chinese open models passed American ones in downloads (1.15B vs 723M by March 2026). The US response: OpenAI's gpt-oss and the ATOM Project, while Meta went proprietary ("the Great Re-Closing").

Why it matters

Openness splits allies (the safety and ethics camps), every regulation fight now contains an open source carve-out battle, and the Tech Right borrows the left's "democratize AI" language. Read any open source claim with three questions: Open what: weights, data, code or brand? Who profits? What regulation is this argument being used against?

Scrambler 3

Surveillance and Flock cameras

The setup

Flock Safety runs 100,000+ AI license plate cameras across the US, an $8.4B company whose lead investor is a16z, which led Flock's $275M Series F in March 2025 at a $7.5B valuation via its American Dynamism fund. Flock integrates with Palantir, which holds a $30M ICE contract. Marc Andreessen joined the Pentagon's Defense Policy Board in June 2026. Nearly 800 cities signed new Flock contracts in 2026.

The blowup

Local camera data flowed to ICE and federal agencies without authorization. Dayton's audit found its cameras searched 7,100+ times for immigration enforcement, explicitly against city policy. An Illinois state audit found CBP had been given access to Illinois plate data through an undisclosed pilot program, forcing dozens of agencies off the state's data-sharing system. Ring ended its partnership with Flock on Feb 12, 2026, after backlash to its Super Bowl ad. At least 53 cities across 20 states had canceled or rejected Flock contracts as of June 2026, with independent trackers putting the figure at 80 or more by mid-2026, including Austin, Evanston and Eugene.

Opposition from the left

"It doesn't matter which company has its creepy cameras in your neighborhood, they all have the same problems: a lack of transparency, oversight, and regulation into how they collect, store, and use our data."ACLU, whose national campaign is called "Get the Flock Out"

The EFF calls Flock's Nova product, which links plate data to personal information, a "surveillance panopticon."

Opposition from the right

Rep. Lauren Boebert introduced the Surveillance Accountability Act restricting warrantless tracking; a bipartisan amendment would condition federal highway funds on banning license plate readers except for tolls; Newsweek now runs lists of Republicans cracking down on Flock.

The schism

The Tech Right's money built the surveillance network; the MAGA base and progressives are fighting it city by city. For AI Populists this is the cleanest "who is in charge" story in AI politics: VC money to police cameras to ICE via Palantir.

Organizing note: unlike most AI fights, Flock contracts are local and cancellable. 53 confirmed cancellations, 80+ by some counts.

Methodology, caveats and open questions

How this map was drawn

Factions are defined by shared beliefs, community infrastructure (funders, conferences, forums) and political behavior (money, endorsements, coalitions), building on Michel Justen's faction taxonomy, with original additions: the political money overlay, the AI Populists faction (from field feedback) and the issue-line analysis.

Caveats

  • Snapshot as of Nov 6, 2026, three days after Election Day. A few races are uncalled, and post-general FEC reports are due Dec 3.
  • Spending totals now use the Transformer tracker's FEC independent-expenditure counts throughout. Earlier editions cited a broader count ($81.3M total, $43.8M to $37.5M), so totals are not directly comparable across editions.
  • Factions are clusters, not cages. Individuals vary; orgs may dispute their placement.
  • Placements reflect public positions and funding, not private views, and do not suggest relationships between groups listed together.
  • Some fights are coalitional, not factional. On child safety and surveillance, the table shows positions, not alliances.

What we're watching

  • 2028 positioning. LTF says 2028 "will show who actually cares." Who Decides, Tomorrow Together and Guardrails are all building toward the same primary field.
  • National security and international cooperation. Not simply US-China competition, but also opportunities for international cooperation on safety.
  • Labor placement. Some trades are pro-data-center jobs; some are funding safety efforts; there isn't a central alignment.
  • Child safety as the preemption vehicle. KOSA, the CHATBOT Act and the Youth AI Privacy Act cleared Senate Commerce on Aug 5, 2026. The open question is what preemption language rides along, and whether the age-verification split splinters the anti-preemption coalition.
  • Tech Right surveillance collision. Its surveillance investments could collide with its own voter base as the MAGA right turns against warrantless tracking.
